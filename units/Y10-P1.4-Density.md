# Y10 P1.4 — Density

**Class:** `10A` · Cambridge IGCSE Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 · three
50-minute singles. Palette: `motion`, continuing P1.

**All three objectives are Core. There is no Supplement content in P1.4** —
unlike P1.3, so the Gold tier is depth and reasoning, not extra syllabus.

| # | Lesson | Syllabus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What density is | P1.4.1 |
| 2 | Measuring density | P1.4.2 |
| 3 | Float or sink | P1.4.3 |

Syllabus wording, so it can be quoted exactly:

- **P1.4.1** Define density as mass per unit volume; recall and use the equation.
- **P1.4.2** Describe how to determine the density of a liquid, of a regularly
  shaped solid, and of an irregularly shaped solid which sinks in a liquid
  (volume by displacement), including appropriate calculations.
- **P1.4.3** Determine whether an object floats or sinks based on density data.

## Synoptic links — these are in the scheme, not invented

Worth making explicitly, because 10A meet all of them in the same course:

- **C1 States of matter.** Particle arrangement is *why* density differs
  between solids, liquids and gases. Density is the measurement; particle
  packing is the explanation. If Chemistry has already done C1, Lesson 1 can
  lean on it directly.
- **Water is the exception**, and both the Physics and Chemistry schemes flag
  it. Ice is less dense than liquid water, which is why it floats — and the
  Chemistry scheme ties that to hydrogen bonding and to life on Earth. Best
  used as the Lesson 3 hook.
- **P2.3.2.3 Convection** is explained *in terms of density changes*. Heating a
  fluid makes it expand, which lowers its density, which makes it rise. When
  they reach thermal physics this lesson is the prerequisite — say so in the
  Lesson 1 plenary.
- **Periodic table trends.** Density appears as a physical property with trends
  down Groups I and VII in the Chemistry scheme, so the word will be familiar
  from a different direction.

## Lesson 1

```
CLASS:      10A
SLOT:       single
TOPIC:      What density is

OBJECTIVES:
  1. Define density as mass per unit volume. (P1.4.1)
  2. Recall and use ρ = m / V. (P1.4.1)
  3. Convert between g/cm³ and kg/m³.

PREVIOUS:   reference/<the last P1.3 lesson>.pptx
THEY FOUND HARD: <fill in>

PRACTICAL:  Demo only. Equal volumes of different materials on a balance —
            a 1 cm³ cube each of aluminium, steel, wood and polystyrene if
            the department has a density block set.

AVOID:      Introducing floating and sinking. That is Lesson 3, and doing it
            here turns the definition lesson into a discussion.
```

**Objective 3 is where the marks go missing**, and the scheme of work says so
explicitly: students convert g to kg confidently and then get cm³ to m³ wrong,
because they expect ×100 rather than ×10⁶.

The scheme's fix is physical and worth doing — build a metre cube out of metre
rules so they can see 100 × 100 × 100 rather than be told it.

So: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Water is 1.0 g/cm³ or 1000 kg/m³.

Plenary link: heating a substance spreads its particles out, so its density
falls. That is the whole mechanism of convection, which they meet in P2.

## Lesson 2

```
CLASS:      10A
SLOT:       single
TOPIC:      Measuring density

OBJECTIVES:
  1. Determine the density of a liquid. (P1.4.2)
  2. Determine the density of a regularly shaped solid. (P1.4.2)
  3. Determine the density of an irregular solid by displacement. (P1.4.2)

PREVIOUS:   reference/What Density Is.pptx
THEY FOUND HARD: <fill in>

PRACTICAL:  The three methods, as stations. Cambridge Resource Plus has lesson
            plans for this — "Determining the density of solids and liquids".
            Equipment: balance, measuring cylinder, displacement can, ruler or
            vernier calipers, a regular block, an irregular solid that sinks,
            a liquid that is not water.
```

Three methods, one equation. The structure of the lesson is: same ρ = m/V every
time, only the way you get V changes.

- **Liquid** — mass the empty cylinder, add liquid, mass again, read the volume.
  The subtraction is the step they forget.
- **Regular solid** — measure and calculate. Watch for cm versus mm.
- **Irregular solid** — displacement. It must sink, and the syllabus says so;
  a floating object displaces its own weight, not its own volume, and that is a
  different idea they do not need yet.

The Gold tier here is method evaluation: which measurement limits the accuracy,
and how would you improve it.

## Lesson 3

```
CLASS:      10A
SLOT:       single
TOPIC:      Float or sink

OBJECTIVES:
  1. Determine whether an object floats or sinks from density data. (P1.4.3)
  2. Explain floating and sinking by comparing densities.
  3. Explain why ice floats on water.

PREVIOUS:   reference/Measuring Density.pptx
THEY FOUND HARD: <fill in>

PRACTICAL:  PhET Buoyancy simulation —
            phet.colorado.edu/en/simulation/legacy/buoyancy
            Or a density column: honey, water, oil, with objects dropped in.
```

Hook: a steel ship floats and a steel nail sinks. Same material, opposite
result — so it cannot be about what a thing is made of on its own.

The rule: compare the object's density with the fluid's. Less dense, it floats.
Denser, it sinks. Water is 1.0 g/cm³, which makes it an easy comparator.

Objective 3 is the synoptic one. Almost everything is denser as a solid than as
a liquid; water is the exception, which is why ice floats rather than sinking
and freezing lakes from the bottom up. The Chemistry scheme covers the reason
(hydrogen bonding and the open structure of ice), so this is a genuine
cross-subject link rather than a decorative one.
