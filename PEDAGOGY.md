# Pedagogy

Why the archetype is shaped the way it is, and the things it should be doing
that it currently is not.

The structure already encodes a lot of good practice — a retrieval starter, a
worked example before independent work, a misconception-led We Do, cold calling
to lift the response rate. This file covers what is missing.

---

## The eight changes that matter most

### 1. Keep the marking phase, wherever the answers live

Answers can sit on a slide or on the worksheet — that is a printing decision
and either works. What must not disappear is **the three-minute phase where
students mark their own work**.

This deck runs You Do for 17 minutes and then stops. That is the loss. Marking
your own work straight after doing it is a retrieval event and a feedback event
in the same three minutes; without it, a student who has been making an error
has practised it for 17 minutes and still does not know.

**You Do 14, mark 3.** If the answers are on the worksheet, the tenth slide
becomes an instruction rather than a list:

> **Turn to the back. Mark your own in a different colour.**

Same phase, same timer, same collective pause. Only the source of the answers
changes.

**Print the answers upside down.** They sit on the same sheet, so nothing extra
has to be handed out, but reading them means deliberately turning the page over
— which is a friction, and more usefully a signal that they are not meant to be
read yet. Rotate the answer block 180°, at the foot of the last page, under a
rule.

### 2. Space the Do Now deliberately

Ten minutes of retrieval is the most valuable block in the lesson, and it is
wasted if all six questions are about last lesson. Recipe:

| Questions | From |
|---|---|
| 2 | Last lesson |
| 2 | Earlier in this unit |
| 1 | Last term, or another science |
| 1 | Previews today — unanswerable yet, settled later in the lesson |

Material retrieved after you have half-forgotten it sticks far better than
material retrieved while it is still fresh. The uncomfortable one is the
valuable one.

### 3. Interleave the worksheet; do not block it

Ten mean calculations in a row teaches the arithmetic but not *when to use a
mean*. The student knows the method before reading the question, so no choosing
happens.

Mix the question types so each one requires deciding which method applies. It
feels harder and performs worse in the lesson; it is retained better and
transfers further. Say so on the worksheet so students do not think it is a
mistake.

### 4. Fade the worked examples

There is a gap between *I Do* (teacher does it all) and *You Do* (student does
it all). The missing rung is the **completion problem**.

On every worksheet, the first three questions of a tier should be:

1. **Fully worked.** Read it, do not solve it.
2. **Half worked.** First steps given, last step missing.
3. **Blank.** All theirs.

For novices this beats three blank problems. The fully worked one is not a
waste of a question — reading a correct solution is how the method is learned.

### 5. Ask them to explain, not only to do

After a worked example, one prompt: **"Why does that step work?"**

Explaining a step to yourself is one of the most reliable ways to turn a
procedure into understanding. One prompt per worksheet is enough, and it should
be on the step students most often get wrong.

### 6. Make them commit before they are told

The Hook already does this when it asks for a prediction. Extend it: anywhere a
result is about to be revealed, get a written or hands-up commitment first.

An answer you got wrong *while feeling confident* is corrected more strongly
than one you were unsure about. That is why the tally on the board matters —
it makes the commitment public and the correction memorable.

### 7. Make the We Do harder to game

"Spot the mistake" works, and three changes make it work harder.

**They commit before the reveal.** Hands up, or written down. An answer you got
wrong while feeling sure is corrected far more strongly than one you shrugged
at.

**Ask what the student did, not what the right answer is.** "It's wrong" and
even the correct value can both be produced without understanding. Naming the
error — *they only multiplied the first term* — is what stops them repeating
it, and it is the same move as explaining a worked step.

**Put one correct item in.** If every row is wrong, the task becomes "find a
flaw" rather than "check this". One row that is already right forces actual
checking, and a student who says "this one is fine" has done better work than
one who invents an error to please you. Say so out loud when it comes up.

Use real errors where you have them. The teacher notes and the previous
lesson's worksheet are full of what students actually did; those beat invented
ones.

### 8. Label diagrams directly

Never put a key or a legend beside a diagram, and never narrate in text what
the picture already shows. Both force the reader to hold two things at once and
join them up, which costs attention that should be going on the science.

Labels on the diagram. Text that adds something the picture does not.

### 9. Aim for about four right out of five

On the first tier of the You Do, most students should be getting most things
right. Much lower and they stop; much higher and nothing is being learned.

If the Bronze tier is producing a lot of errors, the problem is the I Do, not
the student.

## Engagement — what actually holds it

Interest that lasts is not built by making lessons entertaining. It is built by
three things, and a lesson can be designed for all of them.

**Something they can be curious about.** A question with a gap in it — a
genuine "I do not know yet" — beats a fact. The Hook does this when it poses a
puzzle rather than announcing a topic. The eclipse hook works because the
answer is not guessable and matters.

**Something they can succeed at.** Interest collapses when competence does.
The tiers exist for this; so does the four-out-of-five target.

**Something they can see the point of.** This is the one most often missing.
Once per topic, ask them to write where they might meet the idea outside the
lesson — in their own words, not from a list. Students write their own reason,
which works where being told the reason does not, and it helps most the
students who are furthest behind.

A fourth, quieter one: **the unit should have an arc.** Callbacks — this is the
question we could not answer in Lesson 1 — turn six lessons into one story.
They already appear in the plans; keep them.

## Things with a reputation but not an evidence base

- **Learning styles.** Visual, auditory and kinaesthetic learners do not exist
  as a stable trait, and matching teaching to a supposed style does not help.
  Use the medium that suits the *content*: a diagram for a structure, an
  animation for a process, words for an argument.
- **Points, badges and leaderboards.** Rewarding something interesting with
  something extrinsic reliably reduces interest in it. Already banned in
  `GAMES.md`; the same applies to decks.
- **Discovery learning for beginners.** Letting novices work it out for
  themselves is slower and less effective than showing them properly and then
  fading support. This is exactly why the order is I Do, We Do, You Do — not an
  arbitrary house style.
- **Re-reading and highlighting.** Feels like studying, does almost nothing.
  If a revision lesson is being built, build retrieval, not review.

---

## Checklist before a deck is finished

- The marking phase exists: You Do 14, mark 3. Answers may be on a slide
  or on the worksheet, but the phase is not optional.
- Do Now spans more than just last lesson.
- Worksheet questions interleaved, not blocked.
- One fully worked example, one half-worked, before anything blank.
- One "why does that step work?" prompt.
- One "where would you meet this?" question per topic.
- Diagrams labelled directly, no keys.
- We Do: students commit first, name the error, and one row is already correct.
- Worksheet answers printed upside down at the foot of the last page.
- Hook poses a question rather than announcing a topic.
- Every I Do that explains a process has something visual.
